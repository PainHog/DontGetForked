Part One

The Castle

About 20–25 minutes. Have the Entity cards face up in the middle of the table, the map of Hallowby where everyone can see it, and the tracker beside you, set to Suspicion 0 and Turn 1. When everyone is settled, begin.

Welcome

Tonight’s Halloween, so tonight we play monsters. I mean real ones, the kind who have to dress up as people dressing up as monsters.

You all live together in a castle in the woods above a town called Hallowby. It’s a draughty old pile that creaks in all the wrong spots, and right now the larder is empty.

Once a year the town holds its festival, Lantern Night. Everyone in Hallowby puts on a costume and walks the streets with a lantern till dawn. It’s the one night of the year a real monster can stroll down the high street and get complimented on the outfit.

So once a year you go shopping, if you can call it shopping when you never pay.

You have until dawn to steal everything on the castle’s shopping list and get it home. If it goes well, Hallowby wakes up a few things short and blames the children. If it goes badly, the whole town turns out with pitchforks and you run, and if they corner you, you get forked. Which is why this game is called Don’t Get Forked.

The Shopping List

Point to the four places on the map as you read each item.

Pinned to the kitchen door is the list, in spidery handwriting.

  • A cask of red wine, “for the guests”. It’s underlined three times. This one is essential, because without it the castle can’t have a good year. It’s in the cellar of the tavern.
  • A pot of nightshade, purely ornamental. From the florist.
  • Hinges that creak properly. The new ones are far too quiet. From the carpenter.
  • A new cape. From the tailor.

And someone else has added at the bottom, “Also, a mirror would be nice.”

Bring home the wine and miss no more than one of the other three, and that’s a good year, a Win. Do all that and bring home a piece of furniture too, and it’s a Grand Year. There’s a fine gilt mirror at the tailor’s.

Choosing Your Monster

Read the eight introductions, pointing to each card. Then let the players choose.

Eight monsters live in the castle. Each of you plays one, and no two of you play the same one.

  • Dracula, the smoothest talker in the castle. He can look a guard in the eye and walk straight past. Garlic is his trouble, and he’d rather not talk about it.
  • Frankenstein’s Creature, the strongest. It breaks what others pick, and it can carry furniture like nobody else.
  • The Mummy, the cleverest. It’s three thousand years old and remembers every trick. Please don’t tug at its loose threads.
  • The Werewolf, the fastest. If the wolf shows, the crowd just sees a very large dog.
  • The Invisible Man, the sneakiest. Nobody can catch what they can’t see, unless he’s carrying something.
  • A Ghost, who drifts through walls and slips past crowds, though it can’t stand cold iron.
  • A Witch, the best friend to have at your elbow, because her spell makes anyone’s roll better. Her black cat warns her when someone is watching.
  • Jekyll & Hyde, two monsters in one coat. One’s a charming doctor; the other’s a brute who comes out when he shouldn’t.

Pick one each and take its card.

If two players want the same Entity, they roll off (again on a tie): the high d6 keeps it, and the other chooses again. Once everyone has a card, ask each player in turn to say their Entity’s name, so the table knows who’s who. If asked: the Werewolf’s very large dog costs a charge. Tonight’s mirror is Bulky, so anyone can carry it, and the Creature’s Tireless means carrying it doesn’t make its Nimble smaller.

Your Card

Look at your card. Every monster has five traits. There’s Brawn for force and lifting, Nimble for climbing and running, Sly for sneaking and stealing, Charm for talking and passing as human, and Wits for noticing and working things out.

Each trait is a die. You all have the same five, a d4, d6, d8, d10 and d12, in a different order. Your d12 is what you’re best at, and your d4 is what you’d rather leave to someone else.

Every time you roll, you add a second die. You choose which. Pick the Mask, a d6, and you keep your costume on and pass as human. Pick the Monster, a d10, and you let the monster out. The Monster is stronger, but if its number comes up higher than your trait die’s, the town sees what you really are. That raises Suspicion, the whole town’s alarm, by 2.

Suspicion is one track shared by all of you, and when it reaches 9 (8 if there are five of you), the whole town comes after you. Try to keep it low.

A Practice Roll

Nothing in this practice counts. There’s no Suspicion and no charges, and Jekyll stays Jekyll. Ask each player to roll their d12 trait with the Mask d6, add them, and tell you the total. Read the box as far as the Critical, tell each player their result, then read its last paragraph.

Every roll is against a number called the Difficulty, a 6, 8, 10 or 12. The bigger it is, the harder the roll. Let’s say 8.

  • 8 or more is a Success. You do it.
  • 1 or 2 short, a 6 or a 7, is a Cost. You still do it, but something goes wrong, and I pick what.
  • 3 or more short is Trouble. You don’t do it, and Suspicion goes up. If someone was watching, you’re caught and you have to run.
  • And if both dice show the same number on a Success, that’s a Critical, and something extra good happens.

Now roll the d12 again, this time with the Monster d10 instead of the Mask. Did the d10 come up higher than the d12? Then the Monster showed. It usually helps you succeed, but tonight, every time it shows, Suspicion goes up by 2. The Mask never shows.

Let them laugh at whoever’s Monster showed. Then go on.

Your Powers

Each of you has 3 charges. One charge pays for one use of an ability, your Signature or your Gift, and both are on your card. Spend it before you roll. An ability never gets you past on its own; you still roll.

Almost every ability does one of four things. It makes your trait die one size bigger. Or it lets you roll a different trait. Or it lets you roll the Monster without it raising Suspicion. Or it opens a way past an obstacle that only you can take. Your card says which. Jekyll’s Draught is the odd one out, because it changes his form. And when the town is chasing you, any ability can simply make your trait die one size bigger instead.

You can spend a charge on a friend’s roll too, if you’re in the same place. It uses your charge and leaves your action free. Only one thing can make a die bigger on any one roll, though. And if something makes it smaller too, the two cancel out.

Run out of charges and you can still use an ability. That’s an overdraw. Instead of a charge, it costs Suspicion +2, on top of anything else the roll raises.

Your Perk is always on, and free. When the mob is chasing you, it brings your Weakness along. And your Tell is the little thing that gives you away. Tonight, keep the Gift and Perk your card shows.

Castle Duties

Last thing. Everyone at the castle has a job, a Castle Duty, and does the shopping for it. At the place where your Duty’s item is, your trait die is one size bigger on your own rolls there.

Tonight’s list needs a Cook for the wine, a Gardener for the nightshade, a Handyman for the hinges and a Tailor for the cape. The Butler and the Librarian have nothing to buy tonight.

Your card has a Duty already. Keep it, or swap to any Duty nobody else has. No two of you can have the same one.

If two players want the same Duty, they roll off (again on a tie): the high d6 keeps it, and the other picks another. Write each player’s Duty on the tracker. The six Duties are Cook, Gardener, Librarian, Butler, Handyman and Tailor.

Setting Off

The castle door groans open. Down the hill, between the trees, Hallowby is glowing. There’s a turnip lantern in every window and music in the square, and somewhere a bell is ringing for no reason at all.

You straighten your costumes, which is to say, yourselves.

Let’s go shopping.
