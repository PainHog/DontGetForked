Chapter 8

Building a Town

Every raid is on a new town. There are three ways to build one, all from the same parts (obstacles, locations, the way out and the lock-up).

Three Ways

  • A premade town: a fixed set of locations and obstacles, ready to run. Chapter 9 has three, one each for Easy, Standard and Hard, each with its map.
  • Your own town: draw your own town on a map, choose its locations, and build each one by hand with the Building a Location by Hand box below (or roll any part of it). Your map may make some moves take two Turns, and mark some entrances small, even the way out (then a Huge piece can’t be carried out of town).
  • The random tables: roll the locations, obstacles, loot and villagers. A ceiling on the hardest obstacles keeps a rolled town as hard as its choice of Easy, Standard or Hard promises (Placing the locations, step 4).

Rolling a Town

Build a rolled town in this order:

  1. Choose Easy, Standard or Hard (its table is below).
  2. Roll the shopping list before the players make their picks, because they hear it first.
  3. Roll the furniture and the location it stands at.
  4. Place the locations and roll their obstacles (the four steps below).
  5. Roll the Lantern Night custom.

Every town also has the way out (Sly or Nimble, or Brawn the loud way) and the lock-up. Both are the same in every town, and each is one move from any location. Only their Difficulties come from the Easy, Standard or Hard table.

Placing the locations and their obstacles:

  1. Locations. Each item on the list gets a location at one of the three places its kind is found (listed under its kind in the shopping table below).
    • Roll a d3, or pick: 1 is the first place listed, 2 the second, 3 the third.
    • One item per place. If the place is taken, roll again or pick another.
    • If a kind has more items than places, each extra one shares a place already taken (roll or pick), two at most: one location, guarding both items.
  2. How many obstacles. For each location, roll a d20 on the first row of the table below for one, two or three obstacles, which you get past in order. The two ways in count as a single obstacle, the first.
  3. Each obstacle. Roll three dice for each:
    • a d20 on the obstacle table, for what it is and its quiet and loud ways;
    • a d20 on the Difficulty row of the table below;
    • a d10 on the Watched row of the table below.
    For the first obstacle, roll all three dice twice, once for each way in. Reroll only the obstacle table for a way in that is an inside obstacle, then for a second way in that is a second group one or has the first’s quiet trait, until each fits. A later obstacle may repeat one of the ways in (two shuttered windows are fine). Roll one more obstacle at the furniture’s location. It stands after that location’s last obstacle, and its Difficulty is 2 higher than rolled, at most 12.
  4. The ceiling. A town may have one Difficulty 12 (two on Hard). Keep the first you roll, and reroll the Difficulty d20 of each later one until it isn’t 12. The furniture’s obstacle counts by its d20 roll, before the +2, so a rolled 12 counts and a rolled 10 that becomes 12 doesn’t.

Building a Location by Hand

For your own town, or when you’d rather pick than roll:

  • Traits: one or two. With two, usually one is quiet and one loud (the loud way costs Suspicion +1 whatever the result).
  • Difficulty: 6, 8, 10 or 12. Aim for the mix the Easy, Standard or Hard table gives, and keep to the ceiling (Placing the locations, step 4).
  • Watched or not: Trouble at a watched obstacle gets an Entity caught.
  • Group or not: at a group obstacle each Entity who wants past rolls for itself.
  • Two ways in: two versions of the first obstacle, with different quiet traits, neither inside and at most one group.

The Same on Easy, Standard and Hard

  • 3 charges per Entity, 12 Turns, overdraw Suspicion +2 (on top).
  • Local chase: Lead starts at 1, escape at 4, 6 rounds at most; the mob is 8 plus half the Suspicion when it starts, rounded down, at most 11.
  • Final flight: Lead starts at 2.
  • A rolled town needs no map, and no way in or way out there is small.

The Shopping List

For each item, roll two d6: the first for its kind, the second for the item. If you roll an item already on the list, roll both dice again.

The essentials are the first items you roll, one on Easy and two on Hard. On Standard, roll any die: on an odd number there’s one essential, and on an even number there are two.

Only the kind matters to the rules, since it decides where the item can be (Rolling a Town) and which Castle Duty gets its edge at its location.

The Furniture

  1. Roll a d6 on the table below for the piece. Decor counts as furniture for every rule.
  2. Choose the list item it stands with, or number the list’s items from 1 and roll a d6 for it, rolling again on a number with no item. The piece stands at that item’s location.
  3. It stands behind one extra obstacle, after that location’s last (Placing the locations, step 3).

A Bulky piece takes one carrier and a Huge piece two (see Carrying, Chapter 4).

Lantern Night

On the night of the festival, everyone dresses up as a monster and carries a lantern to keep the real ones away. Every town celebrates it in its own way, so roll a d6 or pick a custom for each town. Customs are only flavor and change no rules.

4Pies left on doorsteps for wandering spirits.
5A bonfire in the square, and a straw monster burned at dawn.
6Bells ringing every hour to keep real monsters away.

Running the Villagers and the Mob

From the Designer

This is not a game where the Storyteller is trying to beat the players. You are creating room for chaos and letting the chaos take the table in various directions that will result in fun outcomes. The players dying is the fate of the dice, not the objective for running the game.

Richard Moore

Villagers and witnesses. Everyone in town is in costume and in a good mood, and they see what they expect to see. A watched obstacle means someone is looking, so if it isn’t already a person, give it a face (one of a premade town’s villagers, or a roll). Play the town as busy and cheerful until things start to go wrong.

Choosing Costs. Pick the one that hurts most right now, but never one that costs nothing. A lost Turn hurts most near dawn, a smaller die before a big roll, “drop an item” when the roller carries loot (it drops its own), and Suspicion +1 near the Limit. At the way out there’s nothing to pick, because a Cost has no effect there and everyone still leaves. Always say what happened, such as the baker’s wife taking a second look.

Suspicion. Keep the track where everyone can see it, and let the town’s mood follow it as whispers turn to pointing, and pointing turns to people reaching for pitchforks.

The mob. In every chase, roll the ground on the chase table each round and describe it. Once the hunt is on, the mob is everywhere and keeps getting closer. When a Weakness comes into play, describe it so the whole table notices.

The lock-up. Capture is a setback, not the end, unless it takes the last of you. The captive can try to slip free once each Turn after the one it was captured in, and the others can come to rescue it (see Captured, Chapter 6). Keep the captive’s player, and anyone else waiting, busy by letting them voice a jailer or a villager.

Pacing. Count the Turns aloud, and warn when three are left. Early on, give each player a job that suits their monster, and steer a new player toward a Difficulty 8 obstacle first. Furniture always costs Suspicion +1 at the end of every Turn from the one it’s taken until it leaves town. A carried move takes at least two Turns, so even a piece carried straight to the way out adds at least 1, and usually 2. Warn the party if that would reach the Limit. A raid should fit in an hour or two, and a rolled town is best built before the session, since it takes about half an hour.
